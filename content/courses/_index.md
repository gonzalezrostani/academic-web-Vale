<h2 id="philosophy-title">🌱 Teaching and mentoring philosophy</h2>
<div class="philosophy-box">
<p>I help students turn questions about power, inequality, and political change into arguments they can assess with evidence. My teaching brings together political economy, quantitative analysis, and computational methods through hands-on research. Students work on questions they care about, connect theory to current problems, and learn to explain what their evidence can and cannot support. I make expectations clear and divide projects into manageable stages, with time for practice, feedback, and revision. Whether students produce a policy memo, research poster, or computational project, I want them to leave with skills and work they can develop beyond the classroom.</p>
<p>Teaching and mentorship also mean making research accessible to students with different experiences and levels of preparation. I create room to ask questions, learn from mistakes, and demonstrate understanding through discussion, writing, and data analysis. My work with first-generation undergraduates and the NSF-supported Mobilization and Political Economy REU program informs this approach. In mentoring, I combine regular feedback and methodological training with opportunities to present research, meet collaborators, and explore academic and nonacademic careers. My goal is to give students the tools to analyze data and pursue independent research, along with the curiosity to keep asking questions about the world, whatever path they choose.</p>
</div>
</section>

<details class="disclosure"><summary class="action-btn">📖 Summary</summary><div class="panel"><p>The course introduces social scientists to computational techniques for analyzing large-scale textual information. In an era where vast amounts of text—from policy speeches and legislative documents to social media and news archives—shape our understanding of the world, this course equips students with the methodological tools to extract meaningful insights from unstructured data. Bridging natural language processing (NLP), machine learning, Bayesian statistics, and the social sciences, the course focuses on practical applications in areas like political discourse analysis, sentiment detection, and policy communication. Students will learn essential preprocessing techniques (e.g., tokenization, stemming, stopword removal), text representation methods (bag-of-words, word embeddings), and advanced modeling approaches, including supervised learning, topic modeling, and deep learning techniques such as BERT and large language models (LLMs) like OpenAI and LLAMA. By integrating hands-on coding exercises with theoretical discussions, the course prepares students to engage with textual data critically, ensuring they can apply these techniques to real-world social science research while considering the ethical and methodological challenges of working with text-based data.</p></div></details>

<p class="card-meta">With Chie Togami, Tania Ramirez, Fernando Tormos-Aponte, and Yeneca Lee</p>

<article class="publication-card card" aria-labelledby="mentoring-graduate-pathways">
<div class="card-top"><div><h3 class="card-title" id="mentoring-graduate-pathways">Creating Pathways to Graduate Success: Reducing Barriers for Underrepresented Minority Students in the Social Sciences</h3></div><span class="badge conditional">Conditionally accepted</span></div>
<p class="card-meta">With Tania Ramirez, Chie Togami, et al.</p>
<p class="card-meta"><em>Teaching Sociology</em> · 2026</p>
<div class="actions"><a class="action-btn" href="https://gonzalez-rostani.com/img/Papers/MPE2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">📄 Manuscript</a></div>
<details class="disclosure"><summary class="action-btn">📖 Abstract</summary><div class="panel"><p>Students from underrepresented populations, including women, first-generation students, and racial and ethnic minorities, face persistent barriers in accessing graduate education and professional development opportunities, particularly in the social sciences. To address these disparities, graduate pipeline programs can provide critical support by providing networking and mentoring opportunities, closing methodological and research skill gaps, and preparing participants to apply to competitive graduate programs. This article outlines the principles and goals of a summer program focused on mobilization and political economy by detailing the rationale, implementation strategies, and evaluation framework used to assess its effects. Evaluation of the inaugural program demonstrated the success of a multilayered mentoring approach, tailored curricula, and comprehensive support structures in fostering participants’ academic growth and career readiness. By highlighting best practices and lessons, we provide a template for institutions seeking to develop initiatives associated with greater diversity in graduate programs in the social sciences, the academic market, and, ultimately, higher education.</p></div></details>
</article>
<article class="publication-card card" aria-labelledby="mentoring-engaging-diversity">
<div class="card-top"><div><h3 class="card-title" id="mentoring-engaging-diversity">Engaging Diversity: An Inclusive Approach to Undergraduate Mentorship in Mobilization and Political Economy</h3></div><span class="badge published">Published</span></div>
<p class="card-meta">With Chie Togami, Mariely Lopez-Santana, Tania Ramirez, Fernando Tormos-Aponte, and Mayra Velez-Serrano</p>
<p class="card-meta"><em>PS: Political Science &amp; Politics</em> · 2025</p>
<div class="actions"><a class="action-btn" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ps-political-science-and-politics/article/engaging-diversity-an-inclusive-approach-to-undergraduate-mentorship-in-mobilization-and-political-economy/EA7BCB1ADDA1E121ABF91F3A9798055C" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">📄 Article</a></div>
<details class="disclosure"><summary class="action-btn">📖 Abstract</summary><div class="panel"><p>The political science discipline faces significant disparities in the representation and participation of underrepresented minorities in graduate education. This lack of diversity among political scientists results in a narrower range of questions being explored within the field. Furthermore, the underrepresentation is particularly pronounced in the political methodology subfield, limiting the scope of tools and perspectives to those predominantly shaped by white scholars. This article proposes a template for teaching and mentoring undergraduate students from underrepresented backgrounds to enhance their opportunities in graduate programs. We examine the Mobilization and Political Economy program, a summer research initiative aimed at minority-serving institutions and underrepresented minorities, designed to equip participants with the tools to study social movements, political mobilization, and structural inequality.</p></div></details>
</article>
